Reading the retrieval numbers

What 0.665, 0.617, vec=4 and 0.0164 actually mean — and the three ways these numbers are usually over-read.

Measured 2026-11-02 401-chunk dev corpus gemini-embedding-001

The one-sentence version

Cosine similarity tells you how close two meanings are, not how good an answer is — and in a real corpus the interesting rows sit within two or three hundredths of each other, so the order is what retrieval uses and the size of the gap is what tells you whether the order means anything at all.

Everything below is measured on our own corpus, so you can re-run it (commands in §7).

1 — Three different numbers appear in one probe run

They look alike and mean entirely different things. Most confusion about retrieval comes from reading one as if it were another.

NumberWhat it isRange you will seeComparable to…
0.665 Cosine similarity — the angle between two vectors. Measures meaning, not words. 0.3 – 0.9 for text; unrelated ≈ 0.4–0.5, related ≈ 0.6–0.8 Only other cosines for the same question. Never across questions, never across models.
vec=4 / lex=1 Rank — this chunk was the 4th best in the vector arm, the 1st in the lexical arm. 1, 2, 3 … up to the candidate depth (20) Other chunks in the same arm. Rank is what gets fused; the score is thrown away.
0.0164 RRF score — 1/(60 + rank), summed over the arms that found it. 0.014 – 0.033 (two arms, k=60) Other RRF scores in the same list. It is a rank aggregate, not a similarity.

A fourth number, df (document frequency), appears in the probe too: how many chunks contain a word. df: cost 9 means nine chunks mention “cost” — a word that rare is nearly a proper noun, and the rare-term arm will search it alone.

2 — What cosine actually measures

An embedding turns a piece of text into a list of ~3,000 numbers — a direction in a very high-dimensional space. Texts that mean similar things point in similar directions. Cosine similarity is the cosine of the angle between two of those arrows: 1.0 is the same direction, 0 is perpendicular, −1 is opposite. The length of the arrow is ignored, which is the point — it makes the measure insensitive to how long the text is.

"horse manure"        ●
                              ╲  angle small  →  cos ≈ 0.8  (related)
"farmyard manure"     ●────────●  "borax for boron supplements"
                              ╱  angle large  →  cos ≈ 0.5  (unrelated)
The first over-reading

0.665 is not “66.5% relevant”. There is no percentage hiding in it. A cosine of 0.66 is unremarkable — most of a corpus sits between 0.4 and 0.7 for a typical question. The number only becomes meaningful next to its competitors, which is the next section.

3 — Why 0.617 can beat 0.665

This is the single most useful thing to understand, and it comes from our own data. The question is “Do i need to know where my horse manure comes from?” and the corpus is SAOSO Appendix 2, 30 rows.

Read it twice

The top score went down (0.665 → 0.617) and the right row went up to first place. So “higher cosine = better retrieval” is false. The rendition did not make the corpus more similar to the question; it changed the shape of the field.

But look at the margin: 0.617 vs 0.617. That is a tie. The “fix” is decided by the tie-break, not by a real difference — which is exactly why a single run is not evidence. I told you earlier that the rendition moved the right row to the top; the numbers make that claim much weaker than I made it sound, and this page is the correction.

4 — The case that matters more: six bands within two hundredths

The OPP’s turnover scale, question “what is the yearly cost of membership”. Six rows, six cosines.

Three things this picture tells you, and they are not the things it looks like it tells you:

  1. The bands are interchangeable to the embedding. A spread of 0.023 across six options is noise. Ask a slightly different question and the order rearranges completely.
  2. For this question the lowest band is last. Nothing in the question says how big the farm is, so similarity has no basis to prefer any band — it picks by accident.
  3. With the header prepended (ranked: Above R5m 0.596, Below R250k 0.586) the order flips. Same corpus, same question, different labels. A similarity ranking of a scale is not a decision — it is a coin toss with a decimal point.
The consequence for the product

Which band applies is the farmer’s declared turnover (OPP §2.3 — “Annual turnover is self-declared … subject to peer review”), not a retrieval decision. So the assistant must never let a cosine choose a band. It can present the scale, and it can state the lowest band as a floor because “lowest” is a fact about the table, not a similarity score.

That is why the G11 options are judged on quoting more than on ranking: ranking cannot be trusted here, and no tuning will make it trustworthy.

5 — Why the fused score is 0.0164

Retrieval here runs two arms — full-text search (lexical) and vector search (semantic) — and fuses them by rank, not by score, using Reciprocal Rank Fusion:

score(chunk) = Σ over arms that found it  1 / (k + rank)      k = 60
             = 1 / (60 + 1) = 0.0164   for a chunk ranked 1st in one arm
             = 1/61 + 1/63  = 0.0323   for a chunk ranked 1st and 3rd in both arms

So the numbers are small because they are reciprocals, and the k=60 is there to flatten the difference between rank 1 and rank 2 (1/61 vs 1/62 — a 1.6% difference, i.e. almost none).

What that means in practice: a chunk found by both arms scores about twice a chunk found by one. That is the whole mechanism, and it has a side effect we measured: a chunk that shares no word with the question (a table row of bare numbers) can only ever be found by the vector arm, so it scores at most ~0.016 — while every chunk that matches lexically scores ~0.016 and whatever the vector arm gives it. It cannot win. That is the “vector-only by construction” problem in G11, expressed in arithmetic.

Why we do not just use the cosine instead

Because lexical and vector scores are on different scales and are not comparable — that is the reason RRF exists. But it means the fused list is dominated by agreement between arms, which is a good prior for prose and a bad one for tables. Worth knowing before tuning anything.

Can I set a threshold — “drop below 0.6”?nonothing. Thresholds do not survive a new question, a new corpus or a new model.

And one run is not a measurement

Our own probe suite is stochastic. The ducks question — “if my ducks aren't fed organic food and I make compost from their bedding, will I still pass?” — was run twice today in the same configuration: grounded then refused, the second refusing because the model cited sub_ref 'a' from a chunk that does not store it. Any claim of the form “X made the suite go from 9 to 6” needs five runs per question before it is a finding. (That is the open experiment in G11 Part 1.4.)

7 — How to read a probe line, and how to re-run this

One annotated line of real output

  7. §2.3   [lex8,vec3] 2.3 PGS assessment fee structure (initial certification and renewal) …
  │   │        │  │       └─ the passage's first 90 characters
  │   │        │  └─ 3rd best in the *vector* arm  → semantic match
  │   │        └─ 8th best in the *lexical* arm  → shares a word with the question
  │   └─ the citation locator (§2.3), not the document title
  └─ position in the final budget (the top 8 the model is given)

Read it as: “the eighth-best lexical match and the third-best semantic match, landing seventh overall.” A line with only vec= and no lex= is a chunk that shares no word with the question — important, because those are the ones the fusion starves (§5).

Re-run it

# what the product actually retrieves and answers
PYTHONIOENCODING=utf-8 python scripts/probe_chat.py "what is the yearly cost of membership"

# retrieval only, more budget, to see what is being cut off
PYTHONIOENCODING=utf-8 python scripts/probe_chat.py --chunks-only --limit 40 "…"

# the instrument that grades locators (does the set contain the right clause?)
python scripts/eval_retrieval.py

# a cosine table for any set of texts — ~15 lines, and it is what produced §3 and §4
python -m pytest knowledge/tests/test_retrieval.py -q

The Windows console needs PYTHONIOENCODING=utf-8 or the probe dies on a ● (testing_hazards.md §3.21).

8 — The three questions to ask of any retrieval number

  1. Selection: did the passage that answers it land inside the budget (top 8)? That is a rank question, and rank is the one number you can trust.
  2. Granularity: is the unit right — a row, a paragraph, a clause — or did retrieval hand the model a whole table and hope? A large spread of cosines inside an undifferentiated block is the signature of a granularity problem, not a scoring problem.
  3. Grounding: did the answer survive the audit, and if not, which failure class — NO_RETRIEVAL, CITATION_AUDIT, INSUFFICIENT_CONTEXT? A refusal tells you nothing until you know which one, and one run tells you nothing at all.
Rule of thumb

If two cosines differ by less than about 0.03, treat them as tied and go and look at the passages yourself. In a corpus this size that gap is the width of a rewording, not the width of a distinction.
