Decision artifact · the /ask/ surface

Should the ask interface be a conversation — or is that only what everybody else builds?

We have a working chat window at /ask/. It grounds every answer, refuses rather than guesses, and passes 94 tests. None of that proves a conversation is the right shape. This page holds five alternative shapes as clickable prototypes, all answering the same real question from the same real corpus, so the shape can be judged instead of assumed.

The question for you: which of these should /ask/ be, and what should happen to the transcript archive and the published documents when it becomes that?

How to read this

Five prototypes, one question, one corpus

Every one of the five mocks below answers the same question — Do I need to know where my horse manure comes from? — from the same two Appendix 2 rows. That is deliberate: it is the only way to compare shapes rather than copywriting. Each one also shows what happens to the fee question, because that question is the one our own PRD says we answer incompletely.

  1. The quotes are real; the prose is mine. Every quotation below is verbatim from the shipped documents (Appendix 2, page 76 of SAOSO v2.1, and OPP §2.1–§2.5). The connecting sentences are written for this page and are not recorded model output. Each mock says so.
  2. The interactions are real. Type in the board's filter, click a section in the tree, tick a worksheet item, pin an evidence card. If a control moves, the shape works; if it feels like a nuisance, the shape is the nuisance.
  3. Read the strip under each mock. It says where the links live, where history lives, and what the shape costs in things that do not exist yet.
  4. Part 6 is the comparison, Part 7 is my recommendation, Part 8 is yours. The recommendation is one answer, not a menu.
  5. Then read it at 390 px. A shape that only works on a laptop is a shape for the people who wrote it, not for the farmers who use it.

This is not a proposal to throw the chat away. The five shapes are not five skins of one thing: each one changes what a visitor's question produces. Keeping the current window is Option 0, and it stays on the table throughout.

The five, in one line each

  • A · The answered-questions boardThe page opens on what has already been answered; asking adds to it, and an unanswerable question stays visible as a Waiting card.
  • B · The concordanceThe four documents become the navigation; the answer pins to the clause the reader is already on.
  • C · The answer pageOne question, one URL: answer, full clauses, what it does not cover, and a citation block you can copy into a FORM.
  • D · The worksheetNo search box. The published farm-visit stages become a tickable clause checklist that prints.
  • E · The deskConversation stays, scrollback goes; citations accumulate as pinnable evidence cards.

Five constraints that decide the options before taste does

1 A citation cannot link to its document A citation carries the title SAOSO Standard v2.1. The public route is keyed on saoso-standard-v2.1. Nothing maps one to the other, so no citation on the site is clickable today.
2 Even with a code, the link would download document_file() serves as_attachment=True. Landing on page 76 needs a second response shape (inline) or a rendered HTML clause page — the chunk text is already in the database either way.
3 An answer does not outlive an hour The only reusable answer is a cache entry with a one-hour life, keyed on the exact normalised question. A board of answered questions is a content table, not a cache — that is new modelling, not a new template.
4 Refusals are the only structured question record EscalatedQuery already carries the question, a reason, a status, an answer and who answered it. It is the one place where "a question the documents cannot answer" is a first-class row — and it is also the G6 gap we have not closed.
5 Showing other people's questions is a new decision D59 says we keep transcripts. It does not say we may publish them. A question is free text from a public form: "my neighbour's farm" is a third party. Anything history-shaped therefore needs an approved-and-published flag, not a query.
6 The corpus is smaller than the audience's question space, but not by much Four documents, ~101 pages, ~366 chunks. This is not a search problem at internet scale. It is small enough that the whole standard's structure fits on one screen — which is what makes Option B possible at all.

Part 2 · The shape of the problem

What a chat window quietly claims

A conversation makes four claims about the product. Three of them are false here — not because the implementation is wrong, but because the form asserts something about what an answer is.

✗

“The conversation is the unit of value.”

Our unit of value is a grounded passage. The sentence is pleasant; the clause is the deliverable. A thread that scrolls the clause out of view at the next question keeps the thing that expires and discards the thing that does not.

✗

“The answer is unique to this asker.”

It is not. Everybody's question is retrieved from the same four documents. The ninth farmer asking about cardboard gets the same Appendix 2 row as the first, cannot see that eight others asked, and leaves nothing behind that the tenth can use. A chat is a write-only surface.

✗

“The answer is finished when the bubble ends.”

For a standard, an answer is a locator. The reader has to go to the clause, read it in context, and be able to name it in a FORM or to a neighbour. Our citations are chips that expand into a quote and then go nowhere.

✓

“A refusal is a legitimate answer.”

This one is true and it is the best thing the current surface does. A conversational refusal reads as a boundary drawn confidently; a page of near-miss search results reads as a malfunction. Whatever replaces the chat must keep refusal a state, not an error.

The case that decides it: the fee question needs four sections and we retrieve one

What is the yearly cost to get certified, including the farm visit?

A person asking this has not asked a question about a sentence. They have asked for a set — and the set is spread across four OPP sections on three printed pages.

§2.1 · p.1Annual Farmer & consumer membership: R 100
§2.3 · p.2Assessment fee, six turnover bands: R1 500–R8 000 initial; renewal R900–R8 000
§2.4 · p.3Catering per visit: R30 member, R50 non-member
§2.5 · p.3Seal licence: R500 per annum per farm

Recorded defect G3 Our own measurement says the live answer omits §2.1 — the R100, because §2.1 is never retrieved. A bubble cannot show the reader what it did not fetch. The four shapes that can — a board of related cards, a document tree, an answer page with “clauses near this answer”, a checklist of all four sections — are the four shapes that make that omission visible rather than invisible.

The asymmetry that drives the whole decision. Answers are cheap to regenerate and expensive to find again; refusals are expensive to regenerate (never cached) and the only evidence of a gap in the standard. A surface that optimises for the answer optimises for the wrong half of its own record.

Where the links live

On every card, next to the answer. “Appendix 2 · p.76” is the unit of proof, repeated rather than hidden.

Where history lives

It is the page. Curated, approved, counted. The refusal queue is public by design.

Cost to build

A publishable-question table (question, answer, citations, category, ask-count, approved-by), plus an admin action. EscalatedQuery already has most of the shape.

Strongest case for it

  • It answers the user's request directly: the value of other people's questions is that the tenth visitor no longer has to ask.
  • It turns the refusal into the group's best transparency artefact — a visible list of what the standard does not cover.
  • It makes the corpus's gaps measurable in public, which is what G4–G7 need to be prioritised.

What kills it

  • An empty board is a form with extra steps. On day one every card is a gap, and the first visitor's question is still novel.
  • It needs a curator forever. Curated surfaces rot; a board with a card last verified in 2026 is a claim we have stopped checking.
  • Publishing visitor text is a POPIA decision nobody has made (fact 5). “My neighbour's farm” is a third party.

Cost to build

Highest of the five: an HTML clause browser, a deep-link scheme, and inline PDF serving for the 8 pages that cannot be chunked. Corpus covers 4 of 8 documents (D41: Section B only).

Strongest case for it

  • It makes the product's central claim structural rather than illustrative: the answer is the passage.
  • It is honest about the corpus's shape — including the nine blank sections, which no chat can ever show.
  • It turns /documents/ from a download list into the reading surface that the OPP actually needs.

What kills it

  • A visitor who says “cardboard” can never get to Appendix 2 by browsing: the word does not appear in a heading. Browsing needs vocabulary; that is the whole reason search exists.
  • It is a textbook, and nobody comes to a certification group's website to read a textbook.
  • It does not replace the chat, so it must be paid for in addition to the thing we already maintain.

The answer page — one question, one URL

Answers become assets

Asking produces a page, not a bubble. The page has the question as asked, the answer, the governing clauses in full, what the answer does not cover, the questions next to it, and a block you can copy into a FORM. History is the browser's now, and the site's sitemap.

Can I use cardboard from my neighbour?

The standard does not name cardboard. It governs it as a biodegradable processing by-product, which is permitted only if it is free of significant contaminants, or composted before it comes onto organic land and then confirmed free of significant contaminants. In practice that means you must be able to say what the cardboard is, what was on it, and where it was stored.

The clause it comes from

Biodegradable processing by-products, plant or animal origin, e.g., by-products of food, feed, oilseed, brewery, distillery or textile processing — condition: free of significant contaminants; or composted before bringing onto organic land and confirmed free of significant contaminants SAOSO v2.1 · Appendix 2 · printed p.76   Open the document →

What this answer does not cover

  • Whether your supply is contaminated — the standard sets the test, not the result.
  • Record keeping for inputs (S20.1.1). This page only answers the substance rule.

Where the links live

Two places per page: the clause itself links to the document, and questions next to this one is a real internal link graph.

Where history lives

The URL. A visitor's own browser history becomes the archive, and every answer is shareable to a WhatsApp group.

Cost to build

Moderate: a slugged answer-of-record table (not the 1-hour cache), a route, a sitemap entry, and a decision about noindex on refusals.

Strongest case for it

  • It makes an answer shareable, which is how this group's members actually communicate — and it makes the site findable for the questions people type into Google.
  • It gives the “what this does not cover” block a home, which is the honest counterpart to a grounded answer.
  • It is the cheapest way to get persistence, because the answer is a document rather than a feature.

What kills it

  • A permanent, indexable, generated answer is a claim we now stand behind forever. One wrong sentence becomes a public page the group cannot quietly fix.
  • Two visitors asking the same thing in different words get two pages, and neither is canonical.
  • A refusal at a public URL is a strange artefact — it has to become a gap page, which is Option A wearing a costume.

Where the links live

On every line. A checklist item without its clause is an opinion; with it, it is a rule.

Where history lives

In the visitor's tick state, and in a visit pack they can print. The group's record, not the visitor's scrollback.

Cost to build

Medium: a clause taxonomy per situation, tick state, and a print view. No retrieval, no model, no prompt — which is also the point.

Strongest case for it

  • The group's own artefact genre is the checklist: two of the eight published documents are blank checklists and a third is this prep list. This shape speaks the group's language.
  • It cannot omit a section, because the sections are the interface. G3 is structurally impossible here.
  • It has no model in the path, so it cannot hallucinate, refuse, or rate-limit. It works offline, printed.

What kills it

  • It cannot answer a question. The visitor with an unusual question — the one the chat exists for — is funnelled into a category that does not fit.
  • It front-loads a taxonomy before the visitor knows what they are looking for, which is the failure mode of every government website.
  • It is a different product (guidance), not a replacement for /ask/. As such it competes for the same engineering budget.

Where the links live

On every evidence card, as the document the quote came from. A card without a link is not allowed on the desk.

Where history lives

In the desk itself: a set you can keep, print, or hand to the assessor. Deliberately not a public archive.

Cost to build

Low-medium: the citations already exist; a pin is client state. Persisting a set needs a table. The two-pane layout is the expensive part at 390 px.

Strongest case for it

  • It treats the citation as the deliverable, which is what the group's process actually consumes.
  • It keeps the chat's best property (ask in your own words) while removing the scrollback that hides the evidence.

What kills it

  • A pinned list of clauses reads like a verdict. That is exactly the line the disclaimer forbids — the desk edges towards certifying.
  • Two panes at 390 px collapse into a long scroll, and the desk ends up below the fold.
  • It assumes a visitor who is assembling evidence. Most are not; they want one sentence.

Part 5 · Principles, and where they disagree

Four of our own rules pull in different directions

The repo's principles are the literature of record here, because they were written against this product. Each row says what the principle says, which shape it favours, and whether it actually holds in this case. Where they conflict, I say how I broke the tie.

The refusal is the claim
DESIGN §7.9, D17		 A refusal is a boundary drawn confidently, never an error. Both states are always shown together. 0, A, C Holds, and it is decisive. A and C both give refusal a permanent object (a Waiting card, a gap page) instead of a transient bubble. External principles I am applying — from memory, not from a checked source

  • Recognition over recall (Nielsen's heuristics): show the options rather than require the user to remember them. Favours A and B over a blank composer; cuts against E, which requires you to remember what you pinned.
  • Known-item vs exploratory search (Morville & Callander): a reader either knows what they want or is browsing. Here the visitor usually does not know whether their question is a known item — which is why I do not recommend B alone.
  • Natural-language interfaces win when the input space is unbounded and the user has no vocabulary. That is this audience, and it is the strongest surviving argument for keeping a composer.

Flagged deliberately: these three are recalled, not verified against the source texts. The repo's own principles above are the ones I would defend in a review.

Where the principles disagree, and the tie-break

  • “Data, not code” says B. “Refusal is the claim” says A. Tie-break: a concordance is a better library and a worse assistant, and the surface is called /ask/.
  • “Indexable content” says C. “No unsupported claims” says be careful. Tie-break: C is safe if a page carries the clause and the “what this does not cover” block — the claim is the passage, not the prose.
  • “Transparency” (the OPP) says publish the questions. “POPIA” says do not. Tie-break: publish what an administrator has approved and what names no third party. That is a flag on a row, not a query over transcripts.

Part 7 · Recommendation

A + C: a board of answer pages, with the chat kept as the composer

Make /ask/ a board of answered questions (A), where each answer is its own page (C), each page carries its clauses as links (A’s locator + C’s citation block), and the current chat window survives as the thing you use when your question is not on the board yet.

Why this one

  1. It answers the actual complaint. A chat makes an answer unique, transient and unfollowable. Every one of those three is a defect of the form, and each is fixed by one half of the pair: the board fixes transience, the page fixes followability, the composer keeps the free-form question.
  2. It is the cheapest honest version. EscalatedQuery already holds question, reason, status, answer and answered-by — the board's row is that model, minus the refusal-only constraint, plus a publish flag. The answer page is the same row with a slug. That is one table and one flag, not a new subsystem.
  3. It keeps the refusal as the claim. A Waiting card is a better expression of D17 than a warm bubble, because it persists and it can be counted.
  4. It closes two open items on the way past. G6 (the escalation answer path) needs exactly this row to exist; and the human answer becomes one of the OPP's three voices rather than a dead end.
  5. It does not throw away the 94 tests. The thread becomes the result view of a new question. The HTMX path, the audit, the rate limit and the prompt are untouched.

The order of work

  1. Two prerequisites, whichever shape wins: a code on Citation, and an inline way to open a document at its printed page. Do these first; they are small and every shape wants them.
  2. Seed the board from what we already have. The nine-question probe suite and the UAT scenarios are a starting set of real questions with real citations. A board of nine honest cards beats a board of none.
  3. Add the publish flag and the admin action — approve a question, edit the answer, attach the clauses. Do not publish raw transcripts under any circumstance.
  4. Then the page (C), because the board needs somewhere to put a card's “read the whole thing”.
  5. Then decide about D separately. It is the only shape that works with no signal and no model, and the group already publishes its artefact. It is a legitimate second feature, not a competitor to this one.

What would change my mind, stated so it can be tested:

  • If most questions are novel. If the transcripts show more than roughly half of real questions are not variants of an existing one, the board is a form with decoration and the composer is the product. Nobody has measured this yet — telemetry is P2.5–P2.8 in CORPUS_CANONICAL_FORMAT_PLAN.md, and it should be measured before the board is built.
  • If the group will not staff curation. An uncurated board is worse than a chat, because it looks authoritative and is stale. If nobody will approve cards, choose C alone (answer pages without a board) or B.
  • If POPIA advice forbids publishing visitor questions. Then A collapses and the same content ships as C with anonymised, administrator-written questions.
  • If the committee wants the standard readable as a document — a real, stated need after a certification decision — then B stops being a nice-to-have and becomes the surface, with the composer as a search box on top of it.

What I am not recommending: deleting the chat (it is the only thing that handles an unexpected question), making the whole standard browsable before we can link a citation to anything, or building a worksheet taxonomy that would have to be rewritten for the next PGS group.
