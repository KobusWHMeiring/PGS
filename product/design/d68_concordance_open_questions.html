Part 1 — What is actually true

1.3  Three things the PRD says that my run sharpens or contradicts

(a) sharper §7.5 says the TOC parser “already understands the blank range”. True of the parser, false of the wiring: parse_toc() has no production caller — only knowledge/tests/test_toc_map.py and test_section_parsers.py call it, and the SAOSO parser explicitly skips TOC pages. Showing “10 to 19. Left blank to facilitate future enterprises” is therefore: wire the parser into ingest, store the result, render it. Cheap, but not free.

(b) verified §7.3 says the derivation works for all four documents. The counts are right; two of them are not usable as written. Branch titles come from the first heading in the branch, and that fails in three measured places:

BPP INTRODUCTION / DEFINITION OF ORGANIC AGRICULTURE / GENERAL PRINCIPLES / CONCLUSION # titles the document's own printed table of contents gives (parse_toc, 29 entries) SAOSO 1 · Definitions · p.22 SAOSO Appendix 2 · Fertilizers Conditioners and Soil Conditioners · p.76

These are not cosmetic. The two branches a reader is most likely to consult are the two whose derived titles are wrong: Section 1 loses the word Definitions, and Appendix 2 — 30 substance rows, the appendix the whole “cardboard” conversation lives in — is titled after the first of its sub-tables. A reader looking for wood, bark and sawdust is offered “Farmyard manure”.

(c) verified The corpus is not the whole standard, and the “one screen” framing hides that. SAOSO's stored pages run 22 to 88. The printed standard's table of contents has 29 entries. What is in the corpus is Section B plus Appendices 1–7; what is not is Section A (Introduction, General, Minimum Inspection Requirements — printed pages 4–11) and Appendices A, B and C (93–96). That is 22 branches out of 28 printed sections that carry text, before the ten reserved numbers 10–19 are even considered.

Why this matters for the decision, not just for the corpus

The PRD's strongest line is that the concordance makes the standard's shape legible. Measured, the tree makes Section B's shape legible and is silent about the rest. If the page is presented as the standard, six printed sections have been dropped without a word — which is the same class of failure as publishing a claim the documents do not support.

1.4  A defect I found while measuring, and the PRD does not know about

verified 57 of the corpus's 366 passages (15.6%) share their citation anchor with another passage. The reader disambiguates them with an order-derived suffix (#appendix-2-4); citation links and search-hit links do not, because both call reading_anchor() directly:

# knowledge/reading.py reading_anchor(section_ref, anchor, heading_path) # one anchor per chunk: no suffix unique_anchors(chunks) # same, plus -2, -3 ... for collisions # who calls which unique_anchors -> contents(), concordance_sections() # the reader reading_anchor -> Citation.reading_anchor, SearchHit.anchor # the citation, the search list
# duplicate anchors per document, measured saoso 319 passages 8 groups 48 passages land on someone else opp 30 passages 2 groups 2 landscape 8 passages 2 groups 2 bpp 9 passages 1 group 5 TOTAL 366 passages 57 (15.6%) # the worst groups appendix-2 = 30 chunks # 30 substance rows, all cited as Appendix 2 s1 = 8 chunks # the glossary, by design (test_ingest.py says so) appendix-1 = 6 chunks general-principles = 6 chunks (BPP)

So a citation to any of the 29 non-first rows of Appendix 2 carries #appendix-2 — and lands on “Farmyard manure, slurry and urine”. Today that is a small, invisible inaccuracy in a 366-passage page. The whole point of “View this line in a new tab” is that the reader arrives at the quoted line; the feature makes this defect the product's most visible claim.

And the suffix is a sequential id in disguise

-4 means “the fourth chunk sharing this reference, in ingest order”. build_principles.md §I forbids a sequential id on a public surface for exactly this reason: edit a row into the middle of the Appendix 2 artefact (D53's review path), re-ingest, and #appendix-2-4 now points at a different substance while still resolving — a wrong answer that looks right. The PRD's stability argument (“stable across a --force re-ingest, unlike a chunk id”) holds for section_ref itself and does not hold for the suffix.

Part 2 — The shape of the problem

One asymmetry drives every question below. Everything else is a consequence of it.

A citation names a passage. The tree navigates by section. Those are two identifier spaces over one document, and something has to join them — every time, the same way, in one function:

answer page / board | | "View this line" -> /documents/saoso/read/?section=s5-1-9#s5-1-9 | ^ passage anchor ^ the scroll target v +---------------------+ join: which branch contains this passage? | concordance_sections | --------------------------------------------------+ +---------------------+ | | v | branches, in printed order one open branch | (the pane) +--------> the tree (nav, one link per branch) <---- ?section= may name a BRANCH (then no fragment scroll) a browser never sends the fragment to the server, so the branch must travel in the query string; the fragment is what scrolls. Two mechanisms, one link. Unknown value -> first branch.

Three consequences, and they are the reason for the questions

  • The join is the product. If the tree, the search list and the citation do not resolve through the same derivation, they will disagree about which branch a passage is in — and the disagreement will look like a broken citation.
  • Position-derived identifiers are not identifiers. 15.6% of passages already share an anchor; the reader's suffix is order, and order is not a promise (§1.4).
  • The tree is a claim about the document. It will be read as the standard's structure, so every section it omits is a section the product has dropped silently — and it omits six with text in them, plus ten reserved numbers (§1.3c).

The counter-pressure is the one the prototype already recorded: a visitor who types “cardboard” cannot browse to Appendix 2, because the word is in no heading. The tree cannot replace asking; it can only make a cited answer checkable. That is the whole job, and it is enough of a job.

Part 4 — Evidence for and against each shape

A — keep it flat

  • For: it works, it is tested, and nothing is at risk. It is also the most honest artefact we have: every passage, in the document's order, no curation — which is the disposition §5 of the PRD argues for.
  • Against: it is 333 KB and 366 rows, and the reviewer's finding was precisely that it does not answer “where am I”. Its contents list is one line per passage, not per section, so it is a scroll bar with labels.

B — replace with the tree

  • For: the tree is the printed table of contents, so the page's structure is not a curation. A median visit renders ~3% of the text. It makes “View this line” meaningful, which is the reviewer's own words. One derivation serves tree, search and citation, so they cannot drift.
  • Against: titles are wrong on the two most-consulted branches until the TOC data exists (§1.3b); two of four documents produce a tree that reads as noise (“R500”); a citation can still land on the wrong row (§1.4); and on a phone, 22 branches above the pane is a lot of scrolling before any text.

C — second route

  • For: nothing risks the surface that shipped; the flat page stays as the “everything at once” state for anyone who wants to search and read linearly; you can ship the tree without deciding anything about the old page.
  • Against: two navigations over one document is two derivations to keep honest — the exact failure the PRD §12 warns about. Two addresses split every inbound link, and the citation format picks a winner anyway. It is also the option that most reliably becomes permanent.

D — one column

  • For: a native <select> is the best possible control at 390 px (a real picker, no 44 px rows to mis-tap), it needs no sticky positioning, and it degrades to a plain form without JavaScript. Cheapest of the four to build correctly.
  • Against: it hides the structure — the reader cannot see that section 5 sits between 4 and 6, so the “legible shape” claim is lost. A select also cannot show the page numbers, which is half of what makes the list feel like a document.

Part 7 — My recommendation, and what would change it

Q1 — Replace the flat view with the tree. Option B, the spike's shape. A second route (C) is two derivations over one document forever, and the reason to keep the flat page — “see everything at once” — is not a thing a reader does with 366 passages. What would change my mind: if the UAT judge cannot find their branch at 390 px, the answer is option D (one column, a real picker) — not a return to flat, so decide the phone case before building the sticky column.

Q2 — “View this line”, new tab, with the new tab announced. It is the product owner's instruction, and the argument against it (a phone hides tabs) is answered by the visually-hidden suffix rather than by refusing. What would change my mind: a UAT where the reviewer loses the answer page behind a tab they did not know they opened — then the link navigates in place, and the answer page gets a “back to the answer” link instead.

Q3 — All four documents get the tree, and the two heading-scheme artefacts are fixed in the same slice. A name allow-list is a rule nobody remembers. But the measured titles (“R500”, “PGS SA.”) are a corpus defect that the tree merely exposes, so the slice includes correcting those heading lines in the reviewed artefact and re-ingesting that one document. What would change my mind: if the artefact fix turns out to need a parser change, ship the tree for the two numbered documents and leave the two heading documents flat for one slice — explicitly, with a test that records the exception.

Q4 — Sticky column at ≥900 px, stacked above the pane below it (the PRD's own recommendation, and what /ask/'s aside already does). Add one thing the PRD does not: below 900 px the tree collapses to its current branch plus a disclosure, so a phone shows the text first. What would change my mind: measuring the 390 px render and finding the disclosure unusable — then option D's picker.

Q5 — Show the reserved range 10–19, and say that the corpus omits Section A and Appendices A–C. Both need the stored TOC, which §1.3a shows is one field plus wiring, and it also gives the two wrong titles back (Q3's problem, solved by the same field). The omission note is not optional: it is the claim-discipline rule applied to structure. What would change my mind: nothing on the note. On the TOC field: if the plan cannot afford the migration, omit the range visibly (“sections 10–19 are reserved and blank in the printed standard”) rather than silently skipping it.

Q6 — Stay at /read/. /documents/<code>/ is a download with an established meaning, and every citation shipped yesterday points at /read/. What would change my mind: nothing in this slice. A rename is a redirect and a new citation format, and it buys a nicer word.

Q7 — Indexable, with the document title and the section in the <title>, and a rel=canonical to the ?section= form. There is no sitemap and no robots file, so the choice is a title tag and a canonical link, not infrastructure. What would change my mind: a decision to publish the corpus as an HTML edition would change the canonical strategy — one canonical per section, not per query string.

Q8 — Fix the anchors from content, not from position. When several passages share a section_ref, derive the disambiguator from the passage itself (#appendix-2-wood-bark-and-sawdust) rather than from its index, and accept the old bare anchor as an alias so the links shipped yesterday keep working. The stopgap, if the slice must stay small, is to make citations use the same -N suffix the reader already produces — consistent, but still order-derived, so it fixes the wrong-row landing and keeps the re-ingest hazard. Either way the guard is the same: a test that walks all 366 passages and asserts each one's own anchor resolves to that passage. What would change my mind: if the content-derived anchor collides for the Appendix 2 rows (two substances whose text begins identically) the scheme needs a stable secondary key — measure before committing.

The one thing I would not ship without

Q8's guard test, and Q3's artefact fix. Without them the feature's headline claim — this link takes you to the line — is false for one passage in six, and the tree's titles misname the two branches a reader is most likely to want.
