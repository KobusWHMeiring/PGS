How the code is organised — and why the line moved

Originally the question was "one app or two". Your point about public users graduating into farmers and assessors changed where the line goes, so this page explains both the split and the reasoning behind it.

Companion to PGS_PLATFORM_SPEC.md §18.2 2026-10-18

The answer in one sentence

Resolved

knowledge/ holds the standards corpus as a library — the SAOSO text, its chunks, its embeddings, and the retrieval engine. core/ holds everything a person touches: accounts, roles, farms, visits, assessments, certificates, the public site, and the chatbot itself. core may import knowledge; knowledge must never import core.

That is a different line from the one I first proposed. The first version put the chatbot in knowledge/. Your question about graduation showed why that was wrong.

Why the line moved: graduation

You said users on the knowledge site might graduate into farmers or assessors. That single requirement settles where identity has to live, and it cascades:

  1. Identity is one thing, so it lives in one place. A visitor who later becomes a consumer member, then a farmer, then a peer assessor, is the same person with roles added — not three accounts. That means one User in core, with multiple roles attached to it.
  2. Chat history is personal data. If a person can sign in and see what they asked last month, then a chat session belongs to that person — which makes it user-owned, which puts it in core.
  3. So the chatbot is an application feature, not a library. Chat, chat history and escalations move to core.
  4. What is left is genuinely independent: the corpus itself. A chunk of the SAOSO standard references no farm, no member and no user. It is a document library, and that is what knowledge/ becomes.
Correction to earlier framing

I previously said the two sides would share "zero foreign keys, in either direction". That was stronger than the design needs, and I'm withdrawing it. The right rule is a one-way dependency — core depends on knowledge, never the reverse. A layered dependency is normal and healthy; mutual isolation was me over-selling the idea.

What this means for assessors

You also asked how assessors get appointed. Worth stating plainly, because it changes the model: there is no assessor qualification or accreditation process anywhere in the governing documents. The OPP never appoints assessors — it assembles a committee per visit, and a peer member is defined by knowledge of the production being assessed.

So the mechanism is:

  • Steering committee — elected by members at the AGM, renewed annually, farmers holding at least 50% of seats.
  • Farm visit committee — assembled for each visit by the administrator, and valid only if it has: one administrator, at least two peer members with knowledge of that production type, at least one public member who consumes or buys from that farm, and four people minimum. A technical expert is added if nobody in the group has the required knowledge.
  • Observers are allowed, up to a cap set by the host farm, and must not disrupt the assessment. Visits are open to members and non-members alike.

Two consequences for the build: eligibility is competence plus standing, not a credential — active, paid-up membership, attendance obligations met, and expertise in the relevant production type; and team composition is a server-side validation, so a visit cannot be confirmed if the participant set breaks any of those rules. A farmer's expertise is therefore stored on their membership, and the ASSESSOR role means "eligible to be assigned", not "qualified".

This is also why identity had to be one entity in core: a farmer who becomes an assessor, and a consumer who later becomes a farmer, are role changes on one person — not new records.

Being honest about the trade-off

Option B's case is now narrower than I first claimed. After moving chat into core, knowledge/ is roughly four files. Against that modest size, a second app costs an AppConfig, its own migrations folder, registration in settings, and lint configuration.

I still recommend it, for two reasons: the retrieval engine is the component most likely to be swapped or tuned independently — embedding model, chunking strategy, reranking — and a one-way rule enforced by tooling is worth more than a boundary everybody agrees to respect. And it is genuinely reusable: a chatbot over the SAOSO standard is useful to any PGS group with zero farm data.

But Option A is now clearly defensible, and choosing it would cost you only the enforced boundary — not the design, not the schema, not a single URL. If you would rather have fewer moving parts, that is a legitimate call and it is a small edit to flip.

What it does not affect

  • URLs — identical either way. One root URL configuration serves the whole site:
/                    public site and chatbot
/portal/             farmer account and questionnaire
/assess/             offline assessor PWA
/verify/<uuid>/      certificate verification page
/documents/          the standards, SOPs and forms
/api/v1/             sync and media upload API
/admin/              staff
  • What users see — no page, screen or feature changes. The chatbot and the public site still share one domain, one stylesheet and one navigation bar.
  • Deployment — still one service, one database, one static bundle.
  • The database schema — the same tables with the same columns. Only the file the model is written in changes.
  • Everything already agreed — D1–D10, the delivery sequence, the offline design, the registry migration.

Living together on one website does not mean living in one Python module. That is the whole distinction.
