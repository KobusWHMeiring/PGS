Displaying the table, not extracting it

The extraction problem is solved: the landscape’s three tables are reviewed text in landscape.md. The display question is different. Two of the three tables are processes (Step 1 → Step 2 → Step 3), not records — which is why the grid reads like a flowchart with columns. What should a reader see, and who should control the column widths?

Companion to d76_reader_table_fit.html The spreadsheet demo is live — drag a column edge Self-contained — no network

How to read this

This is a set of design examples, not a proposal. Each one renders the same real content — Step 1, Step 2 and Step 3 of Establishing a PGS group — so the difference you see is the display, not the data. The spreadsheet example is genuinely interactive: drag a column edge, or focus an edge and use ←/→. At the end there is a recommendation and a place to pick.

Your two observations are the starting point: the text reads like a flowchart, and an Excel-like grid the reader can resize feels right. Both are tested below, separately and together.

Part 1 — What the content actually is

Read from knowledge/corpus/landscape.md, not from memory. The shape differs by table, and that is the whole argument.

TableRowsWhat it isWhat it reads like
Establishing a PGS group 3 A process. Each row is a step; the other four columns are what that step needs, how it uses seals, and what it costs. A stepper / flowchart with facets.
SAOSO organic certification 3 A process with a branch. Step 1, Step 2, then a third route (“For farmers wanting direct 3rd party certification”) that has no step number. A flow with an alternative ending.
PGS seals and their use 2 A comparison. Two seals side by side, the same five facets each. A small reference grid, not a flow.

So the observation is right, and it is measurable. Two of the three tables are processes; one is a comparison. The grid is not wrong — it is just doing a record job for process content. The step number is the row label, the resources are a list, the fee is a condition, and the seal is a picture. A reader is not scanning a spreadsheet; they are following steps.

The data already says this. The reviewed block carries the row labels (Step 1, Step 2, Step 3) and the columns; what it does not carry is a hint that the rows are sequential. That is a one-field review decision, not a parser problem: layout: process or layout: grid in the <!-- table --> header, reviewed with the rows. No migration, and the next table can be different.

Part 2 — The two questions inside the display

  1. What is the reading model? A record grid (rows × columns) or a process (steps with facets)? The content answers “process” for two tables and “grid” for one.
  2. Who controls the width? A fixed layout (the reader decides) or a resizable one (the visitor decides). Your instinct — the Excel/Grist feel — is the second, and it changes the fit problem into an interaction problem. The pane is 52.5 rem and the printed row is 153–169 characters (d76 Part 1); a reader who can widen the Fee column has solved the fit for themselves.

They are independent. You can have a resizable record grid, a fixed process flow, or a resizable process. The examples below separate them so you can see which one is doing the work.

Drag a header’s right edge, or focus it and use ←/→ (Shift = 24 px, Home = reset).

What you get: every column is the reader’s to size; the first column and the header stay pinned while the rest scrolls; the type is the same 13.5 px in all five columns (only the step label is bold). What it costs: about 100 lines of vanilla JS, the WAI-ARIA separator pattern, and a persisted width per document/table in localStorage. The repo already vendors plain JS (chat.js) and endorses localStorage for view state.

For

  • Familiar: it behaves like Excel/Grist, so nobody needs a manual.
  • The visitor solves the fee reach themselves; the pane no longer decides.
  • Uniform column type makes the five columns peers, which they are.
  • Works for the seals comparison unchanged.

Against

  • It still presents a process as a record — the flowchart feeling remains.
  • JS + the ARIA resizer pattern is real work, and a resizer that lies to a screen reader is worse than none.
  • Persisted widths are state: a reset and a sensible default both matter.
  • On a phone it still has to restack; resizing is a desktop gesture.

For

Against

Part 4 — The font sizes, which you raised

In the current reader grid the cells are 14 px, the header 11 px, and the first cell’s first paragraph is bold. That is close to parity already; the thing that made the earlier mocks look unequal was the stacked phone view, where the step label became a 16 px serif heading and every other cell stayed 13 px. The fix is simple: all five columns are the same size; the step label is a label (bold, same size), not a headline.

Recommendation: 13.5 px for the grid at comfortable density, 12.5 px in the compact toggle; the header 10.5–11 px uppercase; the step label bold at the cell’s own size. Never a larger first column. The compact toggle in example A is the honest version of “make it fit” — it buys ~10% more characters without shrinking the type below the design system’s floor.

Part 6 — Recommendation

recommend  E — a layout per table, and a resizable grid wherever it is a grid

Add layout: process | grid to the reviewed table block. The two process tables render as a stepper (example C) with the fee as a band; the seals table renders as the resizable spreadsheet (example A). All five columns share one type size; the step label is bold, not larger. The spreadsheet keeps the frozen header and first column, a Reset widths and a Compact toggle, and persists widths per document/table in localStorage. Below 900 px both shapes restack.

  • It takes your two observations seriously. The flowchart feeling is the content, so the process tables get a flow; the grid stays a grid only where it is a comparison. The reader can resize, so the pane no longer decides how much of the fee they see.
  • It is one reviewed field, not a heuristic. layout: in the block is chosen by the reviewer with the rows, exactly like the legend. No model call, no inference from the text.
  • It is a bounded build. A parser field, a second template branch, ~100 lines of vanilla JS and the ARIA resizer pattern, plus tests at 1280/390 and a keyboard test. No migration: the data is the artefact.

What it changes: knowledge/corpus.py parses layout:; ParsedTable/ArtefactGrid carry it; document_reader.html branches on it; reader.css carries the stepper and the spreadsheet; a small reader-grid.js carries the resizers; DESIGN.md §7.13 gains the two shapes; the tests pin the field, the shapes and the fallback.

What would change my mind: if the process cards read as too tall in the UAT (open three step cards on a phone and judge), then keep the grid for the process tables and use the spine (B) instead — the resizable grid is still the base. If the resizer’s accessibility cost is judged too high, drop to fixed widths and the compact toggle, and keep only the per-table layout. If the product owner wants one renderer for all three tables, the spreadsheet (A) is the safe single answer.
