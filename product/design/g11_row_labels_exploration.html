How does a printed column header reach a row’s quotable text?

Decided 2026-11-02 — option E (spec §18.3 D79). Part 6 argued two tracks; the owner chose the target alone, so A is not to be built. Parts 1–6 are unchanged — this banner records the answer, and option E is explained step by step here.

The assistant can now find the OPP’s six turnover bands. It still cannot say R1,500, because a row’s chunk text is three bare cells and the column labels live outside it. Five ways out, and one decision — read together with g12_ingestion_generalisation.html, where option E comes from.

How to read this

This is a decision document, not a proposal and not a design. It exists to get one answer out of you: which mechanism puts a printed column header where a quote can reach it? Everything that could be checked was run; anything inferred says so.

  1. Part 1 is the inventory — what the three table storages actually produce today, measured.
  2. Part 2 is the asymmetry that makes this hard: finding a row and quoting a row are two different mechanisms, and today both fail, for unrelated reasons.
  3. Part 3 gives four options, each with its strongest case.
  4. Part 5 records where the principles disagree, including the one that says my preferred option is wrong.
  5. Part 7 is one recommendation and what would change it. Part 8 is the control.

Beneath the decision there is an open measurement you flagged: applying D73’s rendition to the prose rows (Appendix 2) appeared to cost three suite questions their answers, and the revert is not yet justified by a clean experiment. It is written up in Part 1.4 with the numbers, and it is not part of the four options — it is a separate thing to settle.

Part 1 — What is actually true

Measured on the dev corpus, 401 chunks, 2026-11-01/02. Commands and file references are given so a reader can re-run them.

Every reviewed table in the corpus ends up as ordinary DocumentChunks, one per row. What a row’s text contains differs by storage, and that is the whole problem:

Fixing one is not enough, and that is the asymmetry this decision turns on. A seat that finds the row still hands the model three unlabelled numbers; a header in the text that the model cannot retrieve changes nothing. Each option below is therefore judged on both.

1.4 The open measurement you flagged: the prose rows

D73’s rendition was first applied to all rows. After re-ingesting everything, the probe suite went 9 of 9 grounded → 6 of 9: horse manure, cardboard and ducks all refused. I reverted the rendition for the prose literals on that basis. That revert is not yet justified, and here is why, measured:

QuestionBest row, textBest row, rendition
horse manure0.6650.617
cardboard0.6190.574
straw mulch0.6900.621
borax0.6170.616

Absolute similarity drops slightly every time — but absolute similarity is not what retrieval ranks on. For the manure question the top row changes:

text       best = Source separated human excrement   (0.665) — wrong row
rendition  best = Farmyard manure, slurry and urine   (0.617) — right row
second place under the rendition: Source separated human excrement, also 0.617 — a tie.

And the margin is a tie, which makes that claim much weaker than “the rendition fixed it”: the order is decided by the tie-break, not by a real difference. The full ranked lists for both variants are in product/design/retrieval_scores_explainer.html §3. So the honest reading is “the rendition changes the order; whether it helps is not established” — which is exactly why the experiment below is a precondition rather than a nicety.

And the suite result that caused the revert was one run per question, on a generator that is measurably stochastic: with the revert in place, ducks refuses about one run in two (sub_ref 'a' does not exist in the chunk — the pre-existing G1 class), and manure and cardboard are grounded 2 of 2. So at least one of the three failures was noise, and the other two are unexplained rather than disproved.

What would settle it

Apply the rendition to the prose rows again, re-ingest SAOSO once, and run each of the four affected questions five times in each configuration, recording the failure class (NO_RETRIEVAL / CITATION_AUDIT / grounded) — not just the verdict. About 40 model calls. This is not one of the four options below; it is a measurement to run before the next corpus slice, whichever option you pick.

Interim position: leave the revert in place (it is what shipped), and treat “the rendition helps numeric rows, unknown for prose rows” as the true statement. It matters for option A, whose whole mechanism is a rendition — so if A wins, run this experiment first.

Part 2 — The shape of the problem

Two constraints pull in opposite directions, and both are already decisions in this repo.

2.1 The quote must be the paper’s characters (D69), and the vector must not be (D73)

Two decisions already made, and they are the walls of this room:

DecisionSaysConsequence here
D69the corpus is the document’s characters; nothing is substituted or added a column label the paper prints once cannot simply be pasted into every row’s text… unless it is the paper’s own words
D73quote from text, embed a column: value rendition this separates “findable” from “quotable” on purpose — and only the quotable half reaches a citation

D73 solved finding and left quoting alone, which was right when rows were prose (Appendix 2) and became a gap the day a row was three numbers.

2.2 Precedent already in the corpus: Appendix 2 restores printed headers from reviewed data

D70.1 and knowledge/tables.py already carry the printed part headings (I. PLANT AND ANIMAL ORIGIN) and the column headers for the reader’s shape, restored from reviewed data rather than recovered from text. Nothing there is a quote.

The distinction that matters: Appendix 2’s restoration is display-only (D70.1 says so explicitly). Option A below is the first time the idea is proposed for chunk text, which is citable. That is a real step, not an obvious extension.

Part 3 — The options

Five whole answers, each of which must solve both mechanisms. They are not exclusive in principle — A now and E later is a real sequence — but they are exclusive as the final answer. E is new since the first version of this page: it came out of asking what a general ingestion engine needs (g12_ingestion_generalisation.html), and it is the one the evidence now points to.

A. The printed header line becomes part of each row’s chunk text

Annual farm turnover (gross sales) Initial certification fee (R) Renewal fee (R)
Below R 250,000
1,500
900

Plus the selection fix: reserve the semantic seat properly (or the rows, now having fee and certification in their search_vector, may rank lexically where they did not).

Diagram: artefact — cols: + cells → chunk text = header line + cells → embedding = text (or the rendition, unchanged) → quote = a substring of the paper’s own words, repeated per row.

Strongest case
  • Every word in the quote is a word the paper prints. Nothing is invented.
  • The model reads it correctly without help — the failure in §1.3 disappears.
  • The reader is untouched: the grid renders cells, not chunk text.
  • No new surface, no new claim class, no migration.
  • Measured upside: with the header, the lowest band moves from 6th to 2nd in the vector arm for “what is the yearly cost of membership” — but read §4 of the explainer before weighing that: all six bands sit within 0.023 of each other, so their order is noise and A’s real case is quotability, not ranking.
Strongest case against
  • The paper prints the header once; the corpus then prints it six times. A reader viewing a single row’s passage sees a header above it that is not on that line of the page.
  • A quote may now span header + cells, so a citation can quote a line that is not contiguous on the page — the audit compares against the chunk, so it passes, but D69’s “the corpus is the characters” loosens.
  • Only 6 rows need it, so it is a rule built for the smallest case in the corpus.
  • Chunk text changes ⇒ re-ingest two documents; two tests pin the current text.

B. One chunk per table section for chat; the grid stays a reader shape

§2.3 becomes one chunk — the whole printed table, header and six bands in printed order — the way it was before P4. The reader keeps the grid, built from the artefact alone rather than by aligning row chunks to passages.

Strongest case
  • The passage is a contiguous slice of the page, header included — the strictest reading of D69.
  • The model gets the whole scale at once, so the floor is one addition away.
  • One chunk instead of seven: fewer seats spent, better retrieval odds for a generic question.
Strongest case against
  • Prerequisite: the reader’s grid currently needs one passage per row (align_artefact_grid, core/services/_documents.py:640) and falls back to prose when it cannot align. B means rebuilding that to trust the artefact alone — a P4-sized change, not a row-sized one.
  • Citations lose row granularity: “which band?” becomes “the table”.
  • A 7-row chunk is 200+ tokens of numbers — a weak retrieval target for a question that names one band.

C. Leave the bands to the pages that already publish them

The assistant keeps saying what it says now — the assessment fee applies to every farm entering or renewing, set by turnover, “the specific scale is not provided” — and the answer points at /costs/ and the reader, which both give R1,500 and the R1 600 floor.

Strongest case
  • Zero risk, zero change, and honest: the model really does not have the numbers.
  • The pages that do publish the numbers are reviewed data with citations (§2.1/§2.3).
  • No claim-adjacent code in the answer path.
Strongest case against
  • The owner asked the assistant and got “not provided”. A pointer is not an answer.
  • It is the third surface disagreeing with the other two, which is exactly what G10 was opened to stop.

D. A reviewed fee panel beside the answer, from the data the page already uses

The assistant stops trying to read the table for amounts. When the question is a cost question, the answer carries a small panel built in code from FEE_BANDS and MEMBERSHIP_FARMER_CENTS — the same reviewed constants as /costs/ — with the floor sentence and its exclusions, each citing §2.1/§2.3 as text.

Strongest case
  • The floor is guaranteed, exactly, with no model arithmetic and no retrieval luck.
  • The site and the assistant cannot disagree, because they read one constant.
  • It reuses a reviewed data set that already exists and is already cited.
Strongest case against
  • It is a new claim class: a sentence in the answer that is not grounded in a retrieved passage but in a Python constant. The whole feature is built on “every sentence is traceable to the corpus”.
  • Two sources of truth for the fees (constants and corpus) that must be kept equal by a test — and the corpus already holds the same numbers.
  • It answers cost questions and leaves every other numeric table unreadable; the mechanism does not generalise.

E. The table is shown as structure; the citation renders the row’s context at cite time

The model is shown the table as a table — its printed column headings once, then the rows — and quotes cells only. The reader-facing citation is then rendered from the artefact’s own cols: and the row’s label:

prompt (structure, not a quote target):
  TABLE  2.3 PGS assessment fee structure
  columns: Annual farm turnover (gross sales) | Initial certification fee (R) | Renewal fee (R)
  row    : Below R 250,000 | 1,500 | 900

what the model may quote:  Below R 250,000 / 1,500 / 900      (the cells)
what the reader sees:       §2.3 — Below R 250,000 · initial R1 500 · renewal R900
and what the audit checks:   the quoted cells are in the row’s cells   (substring, unchanged)

Nothing is added to the corpus. The heading is never quoted, so it can never be refused; it is context rendered from structure, the way a citation already renders a document title and a page number. This is the mechanism NotebookLM’s UX implies (see g12_ingestion_generalisation.html): a citation that highlights a region does not need the passage to carry its own context.

Strongest case
  • The quote stays exactly the paper’s characters — nothing repeated, nothing added — so D69 holds without a licence.
  • The label cannot be mis-quoted, because it is never a quote target.
  • The audit is untouched: still a substring check, just against cells.
  • It is the only option that generalises to a table we have not seen: the renderer reads structure, so a merged header, a two-level header or a 1,100-character cell needs no new corpus rule.
  • The reader’s grid is untouched.
Strongest case against
  • The biggest change of the five: a prompt shape, a citation identity (which row?), and the answer/reader templates that render it.
  • A citation now displays text the passage does not contain. That is a new category (“context, not quotation”) and the UI must say which is which, or a reader could think the paper prints the label above the row.
  • It needs the artefact at read time — fine here, but it couples the chat citation to a reviewed file rather than to the chunk.
  • Rows need a stable identity for the citation to reference (today a row is a chunk id).

Part 4 — What the principles say, including where they disagree

PrincipleWhat it saysWhich optionDoes it hold here?
D69 — the corpus is the document’s characters never add text the paper does not printA (and B) partly A repeats a line the paper prints once; B keeps it once
D73 — quote from text, embed a rendition separate what is found from what is quotedA, C yes — and A does not disturb it
D70.1 — printed headings are restored from reviewed data restoration is legitimate, and display-onlyA precedent A extends it from display to citation, which is new
build_principles.md §I — data encodes the standard the fee schedule is data, not codeagainst D fails — D puts the schedule in code as a second copy
Contextual retrieval (Gao et al. 2023; Anthropic 2024) prepend context to the embedded text, never to the quoted text D73, and E agrees with E — the label is context, never a quote
Retrieval for tabular QA (Herzig et al. 2020; TAPAS lineage) a table is retrieved with its header or not usableB, A yes — a headerless numeric row is not a readable unit

The disagreement is real and it is the crux. The retrieval literature says: put context on the vector, keep it out of the quoted text. That is precisely D73 — and E is the first option that satisfies it: the label is rendered, never quoted. A repeats a printed line inside quotable text, which the literature would not do. A tabular-QA reading of the same problem says a cell without its column is not a value — E gives the reader the column without inventing a quote. The tie breaks toward E for a system that must generalise, and toward A only as a stopgap; see g12_ingestion_generalisation.html.

Part 6 — Recommendation

Recommended — two tracks

Target: E. Show the model the table as structure, quote cells only, and render the row’s context from the artefact at cite time. It is the only option that keeps the quote exactly the paper’s characters and gives the reader the column heading, and it is the only one that survives a table we have not seen — which is the requirement g12_ingestion_generalisation.html is about.

Stopgap: A, only if the fees answer must ship before E is designed. It adds a printed line to six rows’ texts, which D69 tolerates and the retrieval literature does not; it should be written down as temporary, with E’s slice named as its replacement.

Do not build both by accident. A changes the chunk text; E changes the citation. If A ships and E follows, E must delete A’s repetition — otherwise the corpus carries a rule built for a mechanism that no longer exists.

Order of work: (1) the §1.4 experiment, because both A and E depend on a rendition; (2) E’s design — prompt shape, row identity, and the “context, not quotation” distinction in the answer and reader templates; (3) branch by layout:, so grid tables get structure and a process table keeps cards; (4) the selection fix in either case — a reserved seat, because no similarity ranking chooses a band.

What would change my mind: if E’s “context, not quotation” distinction cannot be made legible in the answer UI, then A, and accept the repetition. If the owner wants the floor guaranteed regardless of retrieval, then D as well — accepting that one sentence of the answer is grounded in a reviewed constant rather than a passage.

Not recommended: B is the most faithful to the paper and the most expensive, and it re-opens P4’s reader join; C is honest and fails the owner’s actual question; D is a contract change rather than an implementation detail.
