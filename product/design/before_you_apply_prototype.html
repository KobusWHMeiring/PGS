Step 1 of 5 · About your farm

Before you apply

Find out what a visit checks — before you spend a day on a form

Twenty questions in five short steps, about five minutes, no account needed. You get back a list of what to gather, a count of the records you already hold, and the two facts nobody publishes: when your land could be assessable, and which fee band you are in.

This is not a test and it is not an application. Nothing you answer here decides anything, and you can skip any step. The farm visit decides, and it happens after you apply.

Step 1 of 5 · about a minute each

Step 1 of 5

About your farm

Five things about the farm. Bands only — the application asks for exact figures.

A1

What do you produce?

Tick everything that applies.

Why we ask

The group assesses crops, livestock, seed and processing under different questionnaires, and a seed producer should not be asked about compost. Telling us which one you are under means the next page is the right one.

Source: FORM Sheet A

A2

How much land do you farm in total?

Your best estimate is fine — the application asks for the exact figure.

Why we ask

Land area is not used to set your fee — fees follow turnover (OPP §2.3). It is asked because the visit checks field diagrams against acreage (FORM Sheet I §2 b), and because the group reports the scale of farms it serves.

Source: FORM B.1 Q2.1–2.3

A3

How much of that is pasture or grazing?

Skip if none of your land is grazed.

Why we ask

Pasture and meadow have their own conversion period — 12 months before grazing or harvest (SAOSO 4.2.3 b) — which is different from other perennials. Without this answer the timing note would be wrong for a grazing farm.

Source: FORM B.1 Q2.2

A4

How long has this land been farmed organically?

The land, not you — count from when the last non-organic input was used on it.

Why we ask

The conversion period is the most common reason someone is not yet assessable. The standard sets it at 12 months for annuals, 12 for pasture, 18 for other perennials, and 36 where inputs not permitted by organic principles were used (SAOSO 4.2.3). The group’s In-Conversion seal applies for less than 18 months for annuals (OPP §V). We ask so we can tell you which applies — not to judge you. Reserved: if the group settles D31 the bands may move.

Source: FORM B.1 Q3.1

A5

Roughly what was your turnover last year?

This is what the group’s fee scale uses. Bands only — and you can skip it.

Why we ask

Fees follow a six-band turnover scale (OPP §2.3), and turnover is self-declared by the farmer and peer-reviewed — the platform stores the band you choose and never calculates one from sales data. If you would rather not say, you simply will not see a fee figure here; the public table on /become-a-member/ is there either way.

Source: OPP §2.3

Step 2 of 5

Records a visit checks

Do you have these? None of them are needed to apply. A missing record does not stop an assessment — the visit records it and you agree what happens next. What does stop a visit is a question left blank.

B1

Do you have a sketch of the farm?

Layout, buildings, production areas, natural bush, access roads, neighbours.

Why we ask

The visit needs to see the farm’s shape on one page — production areas, water, natural vegetation, and what is next door, because what neighbours do can affect what reaches your land (SAOSO 4.6, Avoiding contamination). A sketch is usually the fastest way to show it.

Source: FORM Sheet I §2 a

B2

Do you have sketches or diagrams of your fields?

Acreage, what is growing where, grazing areas, stock numbers.

Why we ask

Field diagrams are how the visit checks that what you describe on paper matches what is planted — the record is compared against the land, not only read.

Source: FORM Sheet I §2 b

B3

Do you have a farm diary?

Plantings, harvest quantities, anything you apply to the land.

Why we ask

A diary is the evidence trail behind everything else you tell us: what was planted, what was harvested, what was applied. It is also what makes an input/output reconciliation possible at all.

Source: FORM Sheet I §2 c

B4

Do you have records of the inputs you buy or receive?

Including seeds and seedlings, and donations as well as purchases.

Why we ask

Inputs are where a visit starts, because that is where an organic claim is easiest to lose: one treated seedling or one bag of conventional compost changes what the land has received. The evidence needed is what was bought and from where. The group does not ask what you paid, and it does not ask for your supplier contracts.

Source: FORM Sheet I §2 d

B5

Do you have sales records?

Whatever form they take — a book, slips, a spreadsheet, invoices.

Why we ask

Sales records are how the visit shows that produce sold as yours was grown by you — the same traceability requirement that applies to bought-in produce (OPP §VI.3). The group does not ask what you earned, and the figure it looks for is the quantity, not the price.

Source: FORM Sheet I §2 e

B6

Do you have an input/output reconciliation?

Your sales compared against what you produced, to show nothing came in from outside.

Why we ask

This is the one comparison that proves the two halves agree: what you produced against what you sold. Where it does not exist, the group can help set one up — it is a record you build forward, and there is no requirement that you have been keeping it.

Source: FORM Sheet I §2 f

B7

Do you have soil and water test results?

Including soil organic matter and cation exchange capacity, if your lab reports them.

Why we ask

A soil test is a snapshot of soil health, not a pass mark, and neither the standard nor the assessment form sets a maximum age. A test from a few years ago is still a starting point — tell the visit when it was taken. A missing test is recorded, not held against you.

Source: FORM Sheet I §2 (unnumbered line)

Step 3 of 5

Practices the visit looks at

How you farm, in five questions the visit asks too.

C1

Do you make your own compost?

Why we ask

The FORM’s verdict V4 describes adequate on-farm composting as "a key requirement of organic principles", so it is one of the first practices a visit looks at.

Source: FORM B.1 Q4.2

C2

Do you buy in compost?

Purchased compost has to come from an acceptable source.

Why we ask

Purchased compost is allowed, but its source and quality are checked (FORM V5) — the risk is what came in with it. The supplier list belongs in the application, not here.

Source: FORM B.1 Q5.1

C3

Where do your seeds come from?

Tick the one that is most of it.

Why we ask

The standard prefers organically produced seed, allows in-conversion material, and then permits conventional seed provided it has not been treated with prohibited post-harvest pesticides (SAOSO 4.1.1). So "a mix" or "conventional untreated" does not disqualify you. What the visit needs later is the status of each seed lot, because the FORM uses that as the baseline for improvement — which is why one word is enough here and not enough then.

Source: FORM B.1 Q11.1

C4

Are all your seeds non-GMO?

If you buy conventional seed, the group will ask for a letter from the supplier saying the seed is not treated and not GMO.

Why we ask

The standard does not permit ingredients, additives or processing aids derived from GMOs (SAOSO 2.3.2), and the group can take samples for laboratory analysis (SAOSO 1.4.1). "Some are, some aren’t" is a common and answerable answer — it becomes a list of what to replace, not a refusal.

Source: FORM B.1 Q11.2

C5

Do you buy in produce from other farmers to sell on?

Buying in other people’s produce is allowed, with conditions.

Why we ask

Buying in produce is allowed, and OPP §VI sets the conditions: the group assesses each case, every supplying farm signs a declaration and commits to progressing towards certification within six months, traceability runs through invoices and delivery notes, organic and conventional produce are physically separated, and batches are labelled by production method (OPP §VI.1–6.5). A "yes" changes what the visit checks, not whether you can join.

Source: FORM Sheet A, Aggregation

Step 5 of 5

Two consents

Both are unticked. Nothing is stored unless you tick the first one.

Why we ask

The group is asked by PGS South Africa and SAOSA what farmers are looking for and what stands in the way, and today it can only answer from the farms that already joined — which is the one group guaranteed to be positive about joining. Your answers, in totals, are what makes that answer honest.

Source: D64, 2026-10-23

Why we ask

The assessment form collects gender, age group and demographic group and states the purpose on the form itself: "for census purposes — PGS SA & SAOSO collect this data to profile small-scale organic farmers in South Africa for advocacy & support purposes". That is the whole of what it is for. Funding applications are not covered by this consent — they would need different information and a separate agreement (Part 6A).

Source: FORM Sheet A, census; POPIA special information
