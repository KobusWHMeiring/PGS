D1 — What shape is MVP 1?

The delivery sequence bundles a whole category of work into a single slice. Two days of planning follow from how that slice is cut.

§19, slice 1: “Offline visit. Application → pre-visit questionnaire → offline walkabout → split-sync → Sheet J PDF.”

Is that one deliverable, or two that happen to be shipped together?
Spec §19, §7–§11 PRD §9 theme D Depends on D2 — the spike 2026-10-18

How to read this

This is a decision document, not a proposal deck. It sets out what is actually inside slice 1, why the two halves fail differently, three ways to cut it, the evidence for and against each, and the architecture principles that bear on it — including the ones that argue against the recommendation.

The recommendation is in Part 7. The reason it matters is at the top of Part 2, and it is not what §19 assumes. If you only read one section, read that one.

Part 5 deliberately shows the software literature disagreeing with itself. If a document of this kind resolves a genuinely close call by citing principles that all point one way, it is not analysing — it is rationalising.

Nothing here is decided. Part 8 records your call and exports it for the spec’s decision log.

Part 2 — The shape of the risk

This is the observation that should drive the decision, and it is not stated in §19.

The two halves fail for unrelated reasons

Breadth half — the visit flowDepth half — the field client
Fails when… the FORM’s 91 verdicts don’t fit one schema: inverted polarities, duplicate V32, a legitimate “needs In-Conversion” answer that looks like a failure iOS evicts IndexedDB mid-visit, quota is exhausted by photos, or a replay creates a second audit
Discovered by reading the workbook and writing tests a real phone in a real dead zone — nothing else
Feedback loop seconds (pytest)days (device, travel, signal)
Schema impact all of it — 16 tables, and Assessment.template_schema is frozen as evidentiary history none — SyncReceipt and EvidencePhoto belong to the breadth half and are already specified
Cut line if it overruns drop categories (Ship Crops only) no natural cut — the feature is binary: it works in the field or it does not
The consequence

All of the irreversible risk sits in the breadth half, and all of the unknowable risk sits in the depth half. That is an argument for sequencing them deliberately rather than for either bundling or fully separating them — see Part 7.

⚑

An internal inconsistency in §19. §19 says the order is “by risk retired per unit of effort, not by ease of building.” Slice 1 then sequences application, questionnaire, scheduling and PDF before the offline walkabout — which is breadth-first, not risk-first. The hardest thing is last inside the hardest slice. Either the principle or the ordering needs to change; they cannot both stand.

One more thing §19 does not mention

Flow B — the pre-visit questionnaire — is specified as deliberately online-only: “This is an online-first form — it is not part of the offline PWA.” So slice 1 already contains a substantial component that is explicitly excluded from the offline requirement. The slice is composite by its own definition; nothing about it is uniformly “offline”.

Part 3 — Three ways to cut it

Not two options. The interesting distinction is between splitting the build and splitting the claim.

A

One slice — build and ship together

Exactly as §19 writes it. Breadth and depth built in one effort; one gate: an assessor completes a visit with no signal.

One work package, one ship gate

Domain + flows

Sync API
contract
Field client

B

Two slices, two ship gates

MVP 1 = the online visit flow with a degraded capture form usable where there is signal. MVP 1b = the offline client. Two independent claims.

Two work packages, two ship gates

Domain + flows + PDF
MVP 1 ships
Sync API + field client
MVP 1b ships

Requires extra scope to be honest: the degraded capture form is not free. Without it, 1a means paper in the field plus digital re-entry afterwards — more work than today, and precisely the “paper reconciliation step” §1 exists to remove.

C

Two increments, one ship gate — the seam is a contract

Split the building, not the claim. A versioned seam contract is written and tested before either half starts. 1a is demonstrable internally; the MVP 1 statement — “a visit can be run paperlessly” — is only made when 1b passes a field test.

Two increments, a frozen contract at the seam

1a — breadth
  • 16 models · flows A, B, E

  • Sheet J PDF

  • sync API + idempotency
Seam contract v1 — frozen before either half starts visitPack.schema.json VisitSyncEnvelope v1 client_uuid client_media_uuid
contract tests run against both sides
1b — depth
  • 0 new tables
  • service worker + cache
  • IndexedDB stores
  • offline capture + quota
  • pack consumer · 3-phase sync

What the seam actually carries

The three-phase protocol is why the contract must be written down rather than assumed. Text always precedes media; finalisation always follows both; and a replay must create zero additional rows. Those are properties of the envelope, not of either tier — so they belong to the seam.

Split-sync, three phases — idempotency is the load-bearing property

Ordering is enforced by both sides: text before media, finalisation after both. A server that trusts the client's ordering is not enforcing it.

Part 4 — Evidence for and against

Each option gets the strongest case that can be made for it. Where an argument survives only on an assumption, the assumption is named.

A

One slice

The case §19 implicitly makes, assembled properly.

For
  1. The end-to-end claim is the product. §1’s success statement is “zero retyping by the administrator”. That only becomes true when both halves land, so a half-built slice delivers no part of the promise.
  2. The contract is discovered, not designed. The pack format is the real risk. Designed without its consumer, it will suit server-side rendering and fail offline — per-question photo association, verdict polarity, resumable drafts.
  3. One freeze of the most sensitive table. Assessment.template_schema is evidentiary and immutable. Every schema migration before it settles is churn in the one place you least want it.
  4. A forcing function against drift. Bundled, the risky half cannot be quietly deferred once the safe half is “done”.
  5. A half-visit is not usable by the group. They cannot run a single real visit on the breadth half alone.
Against
  1. No cut line. If the field client overruns — and it is the part nobody can estimate — there is no natural point to descope; you are simply late.
  2. Interleaved debugging. A failure could be domain modelling, browser storage, or the contract between them. Separating builds separates the search space.
  3. It contradicts §19’s own ordering principle (Part 2).
  4. Its strongest argument rests on an assumption: that the contract cannot be discovered early. It can — by defining it explicitly and testing both sides against it, which is what Option C does.
B

Two slices, two claims

The argument from delivery and risk isolation.

For
  1. Risk isolation and a real cut line. The novel engineering gets its own budget and its own overrun.
  2. Asymmetric automated confidence. The field client can only be trusted after real-device testing; isolating what you cannot test keeps the fast feedback loop fast for everything else.
  3. Risk-first, properly. Puts the unknowable before the merely laborious — which is what §19 claims to do.
  4. Learning before freezing. You find out whether the FORM survives a schema before the schema is frozen by offline constraints too.
  5. “Working, simple, then evolved” — a working online capture form is a simple system that works.
Against
  1. It needs scope it does not admit to. Without a degraded capture form, MVP 1 makes the group’s work worse: paper in the field, then digital re-entry. That is extra scope, not a free win.
  2. It makes a claim that is not true yet. Declaring “MVP 1 shipped — visits are digital” when the dead-zone case is unbuilt is the kind of half-truth this project exists to avoid; the same discipline that keeps MVP 0 claim-free applies here.
  3. The seam still has to be designed. B does not remove the contract problem, it just discovers it later — with 1a already built against a guess.
  4. Two release cycles for a group that has not yet run one.
C

Two increments, one claim

The synthetic option: take A’s contract argument and B’s risk isolation, refuse B’s claim.

For
  1. Keeps A’s best argument — the contract is discovered with both consumers in view — by making it explicit and testing both sides against it.
  2. Keeps B’s risk isolation, with a work-package boundary and separate estimates rather than separate announcements.
  3. Honest inventory: 1b needs zero new tables (Part 1). The irreversible risk really is in 1a, so the seam is a genuine module boundary rather than a bookkeeping device.
  4. Preserves optionality: if the spike fails, only 1b’s approach changes; the contract absorbs it (a file-export variant is a v2 envelope, not a rewrite of the domain).
  5. No false claim and no wasted work — 1a is genuinely required by 1b, so neither increment can be descoped into uselessness.
Against
  1. More planning ceremony — a written schema, contract tests, versioning discipline, before any user-visible progress.
  2. If the seam contract is wrong you pay twice — though in that case A pays the same price with less warning.
  3. Still one long gate. The group still waits for the whole thing, so C is strictly slower to first value than B.
  4. It is a middle position, and middle positions can be the sum of both options’ costs rather than their intersection.

Part 5 — What the architecture literature says

The principles do not agree. That is the honest finding, and it is why this needs a judgement rather than a lookup.

PrincipleWhat it saysFavours
Walking skeleton
Cockburn		 Build the thinnest implementation that runs end-to-end through every layer, early. It is the cheapest way to validate that the architecture holds together. A
Gall’s law A complex system that works evolves from a simple system that works. You cannot build a working complex system in one pass. B / C
Spiral / risk-driven order
Boehm		 Attack the highest-risk item first, because it is the one that can invalidate the plan. C
Last responsible moment
Lean / Poppendieck		 Defer irreversible commitments until you have the most information you can cheaply get. The template schema freeze is exactly such a commitment. C
Information hiding
Parnas		 Module boundaries should hide the decisions most likely to change. The sync envelope hides the storage strategy — so it should be an explicit interface, not an implicit one. C
Integration risk
incremental delivery		 Big-bang integration of two large halves late is where projects fail. Integrate continuously against a contract. B / C
Optimise for cost of change Put the effort where change is expensive. Table stakes: the schema. Field client: cheap to change, expensive to verify. A / C
Testability / feedback loop Systems you cannot test quickly should be isolated so their slow loop does not slow everything else. B / C
Conway’s law
weakened here		 Architecture mirrors communication structure. With one developer there is no team boundary — but the cognitive version survives: you can hold one hard problem at a time. B
YAGNI Do not build the contract and its harness before you need them. A
Reading the disagreement

Walking skeleton and YAGNI point to A. Six principles point to C. The tie-break is a fact about this system rather than a principle: the walking skeleton argument assumes the skeleton is thin, and MVP 1 is not — it is 16 tables, four flows, a PDF renderer and a novel browser client. A skeleton that thick is not a skeleton.

Why the spike gates the decision

D1 and D2 are not independent. The spike’s outcome changes what 1b is, and therefore what the seam contract must carry:

Spike resultWhat it means for the seamEffect on this decision
Persisted storage holds; drafts survive a kill Envelope carries text + media as designed C as written. Option A becomes defensible too, since its main risk is retired.
Eviction or quota kills long sessions Envelope needs checkpointing, or a file-export fallback (envelope v2) C is now clearly right — the contract absorbs a design change that would otherwise land after 1a was built against a guess.
Offline is not viable on the target devices at all No envelope; a different capture strategy entirely B becomes correct — ship the online flow, re-plan the field story.
What this changes in practice

§19 currently lists the spike as “0∥ — parallel”. That is too weak. Running it in parallel with MVP 0 is fine as scheduling, but the seam contract must not be frozen before its result is known. Two days of spike protects the single most expensive-to-change artefact in the slice.

And §19’s “risk retired per unit of effort” ordering should be re-stated honestly: either the slice becomes depth-first (spike → field client → breadth), or the principle is replaced with “user-visible value first”. Keeping both is a small inconsistency that will be used later to justify either choice.

What would change my mind

If…Then
The spike says offline is straightforward on the target devices A is cheaper and C’s ceremony is not worth it — build one slice, keep the contract informal.
The group needs something running within two months B, but only with the degraded capture form explicitly budgeted — otherwise you ship extra work, not less.
Authoring the templates from the FORM turns out to be trivial The irreversible risk shifts entirely to the field client and C collapses toward B.
There will be a second developer C becomes mandatory: two people on one bundle without a contract is a merge conflict with a deadline.
