1.2  Where it breaks — the pipeline, with the damage located

verified Every page goes through the same four stages. The stage that breaks this document is step 2, and it breaks it because the page is not what step 2 assumes:

pgs_docs/PGS Certification landscape_Final.pdf | | (1) pdftotext -layout -enc UTF-8 -- the corpus contract; the page as a grid v raw page text (10 pages) | | (2) knowledge/columns.py :: de_column -- assumes TWO COLUMNS OF PROSE | detects 4 gutters on PDF 4 -- emits 4 column blocks, in order v re-ordered page text <=== THE ROW IS DESTROYED HERE | | (3) knowledge/sections/heading.py -- opens a section at a CAPS line | "R500" alone on a line -> a heading -- invents a branch, cuts the row v 8 sections -> 8 chunks -> 6 concordance branches | | (4) reader / chatbot -- a cell is never a contiguous substring v "establishing a pgs group" = one third of a table

Step 2 is not a bug in the sense of a coding error. It is doing exactly what it was built to do, to a page built for something else.

1.4  No extraction mode makes a cell quotable — this is the load-bearing finding

verified Five sentences that are verbatim contiguous on the paper, checked as flat(sentence) in flat(chunk) across every chunk of the document, for three renderings:

RenderingPaper sentences quotableWhy
A. raw -layout1 / 5a row’s cells sit on one visual line, so a cell’s sentence is interrupted by its neighbour
B. de_column (shipped)2 / 5slightly better by accident: some lines span a band and are kept whole
C. pdftotext -table for PDF 4–80 / 5rows align, but columns still share a line — and the footer stops being suppressed, so furniture becomes headings

And C is loss-free: its non-space character multiset is identical to A’s on every page that carries text (2–9, measured). So the best available extraction still scores 0 of 5. Tuning the detector cannot fix this, because the problem is not detection:

Why this cannot be extracted away

A table row is a two-dimensional object. Text is a sequence. Every extraction mode must flatten one axis, so either the columns interleave or the rows are torn apart. The repo already wrote this down in CORPUS_COLUMN_EXTRACTION_PRD.md:

“Not in scope: … table row reconstruction.”

1.5  The finding that is bigger than the tables: pages 1–3 are not in the corpus at all

verified Measured on the live corpus and reproduced from a fresh in-process build. Four probes, four zeros:

# does any chunk of the landscape contain ... Participatory Guarantee Systems (PDF 2) -> 0 six basic elements (PDF 2) -> 0 First-Party (PDF 3) -> 0 Third-Party (PDF 3) -> 0

The 8 chunks cover PDF 4–9. Step 3 of the pipeline opens a section at a CAPS line, and pages 1–3 print none that survive: page 2’s only capitals line is the running header, which is suppressed by repetition; page 3’s First-Party Claim / Third-Party Certification are Title case, which heading.py documents as a deliberate miss (“a false heading is a worse citation than a missing one”). Page 1 is a cover; page 10 is blank.

What this means in product terms

The plain-language answer to “what is a PGS?”, the six basic elements, and the three certification routes are exactly what a smallholder asks about, and none of it is citable — not because the text is scrambled, but because there is no row in DocumentChunk. A retrieval miss here is indistinguishable from a corpus gap, which is the failure mode testing_hazards.md §1.17 records as the expensive one. Two of those three routes are also the subject of the public site’s claim-discipline rules.

It is not an extraction problem: the text extracts fine. The parser has no section to put it in.

Part 2 — The shape of the problem

Why this is not a bug to fix but a boundary to cross, and the asymmetry that decides which options are worth the page.

2.1  The asymmetry: reading order and row association are opposites

A human reads a table by jumping between two axes. A chunk is one axis. That is why the measured score in §1.4 is 0–2 of 5 for every mode, and why the fix cannot be a better mode. The only object that satisfies both axes is one that was decided, not derived:

what the paper means what a chunk can hold ----------------------------- -------------------------------- row 1 [A|B|C|D|E] <-- 2-D "A B C D E" <-- 1-D, one row row 2 [F|G|H|I|J] "F G H I J" row 3 [K|L|M|N|O] "K L M N O" pairing (which B goes with A) the pairing, once decided, IS the row is on the paper, not in text (a chunk per row = a quotable cell)

2.2  The decision table — under which conditions does the answer change?

2.3  What the corpus contract actually forbids

The artefact contract (D53, knowledge/corpus.py) says every block is a contiguous slice of the pages its page marker names. A table row is a reconstruction — which is why tables.py carries the single documented exception to “a chunk is a slice”, and why the exception is reviewed data rather than a cleverer extractor:

“The row text joins two cells; the join itself is not on the paper. A quote may cover either cell or the whole row, and a reader checks it by finding the row, not a sentence. This is the deliberate exception to chunking.py’s ‘a chunk is always section.text[start:end]’.”

— knowledge/tables.py, assumption 3

Part 3 — The options

Five options for the mechanism as a whole. Q1–Q7 then decide the parts that any of them still leaves open.

A  — Reviewed rows in tables.py (data + a guard)

human reads the page -> TABLE_ROWS[(doc, branch)] -> 1 chunk per row ^ | | v test pins each cell reader: labelled cells to its printed line chat: quotable cell

What Appendix 2 and Appendix 6a already do. Generalised to N cells and keyed by document.

B  — A vision model drafts the table, at ingest

page image -> model -> JSON -> DocumentChunk no human in the loop

The model is good at this. The question is not its accuracy; it is what happens to the corpus’s traceability contract when a chunk is not a slice of any page.

C  — A layout library or a per-table parser

page -> pdfplumber / camelot / custom geometry -> rows inferred pairing

Infers the pairing from geometry. Works until the geometry is unusual, which is the situation that produced this page.

D  — Markdown tables in the canonical artefact

knowledge/corpus/landscape.md | Key steps | Resource documents | Use of seals | Seals | Fee | |-----------|--------------------|--------------|-------|-----| | Step 1 | IFOAM PGS tool kit | ... | ... | ... |

One reviewed file per document, tables and all. The long-term shape if the number of tables grows; a format version, a generator change and a new equivalence test if it happens now.

E  — Exclude the pages and publish the gap

PAGE_LAYOUT_EXCLUSIONS["landscape"] = {4,5,6,7,8} -> printed grid preserved, rows still unquotable, reader states the limitation

Cheapest. Honest. Does not answer “what does membership cost?”

Part 4 — Evidence for, then against

Each option gets its strongest case before its weaknesses.

A  — Reviewed rows

For

  • It is the only option measured to make a cell quotable. The mechanism already delivers this for 50 SAOSO rows.
  • It is the plan of record. G4 assigns exactly this work, and CORPUS_CANONICAL_FORMAT_PLAN.md answer 4 assigns pairing to tables.py rather than to the artefact.
  • It fits what a human must do anyway. 8 rows is twenty minutes of reading, once per edition. The alternative is a permanent heuristic for a document that will not change.
  • It can fail loudly. A reprint that moves a column fails a test instead of moving a fee onto the wrong seal.

Against

  • It puts document content in Python. The row text lives in a module, not in the artefact — two files to keep consistent, and a reviewer who must diff both.
  • It needs the key change. rows_for("Appendix 2") is global; the landscape has section_ref = NULL. chunks_for_sections must learn the document, and that touches corpus_check and the reader’s call site.
  • It does not scale to “every table in every standard”. At some number of curated tables, option D becomes the cheaper review.

B  — Vision model at ingest

For

  • Zero human time per table, and it handles seals, merged cells and page breaks better than any mode in §1.4.
  • The repo has already allowed the drafting half of it — D70.2 keeps a model in the loop as a drafter.

Against

  • It breaks the traceability contract, which is the whole point of D53. An unreviewed chunk is not a slice of any page, so “this quote is on printed page 6” stops being provable.
  • The failure is silent and quotable. A wrong digit in R500 passes every guard, reaches a farmer, and looks exactly like the paper.
  • It cannot be tested cheaply. The existing guard style — pin each cell to its printed line — exists precisely so the answer is falsifiable.
  • It adds a network dependency to ingest. The corpus build is currently offline, deterministic and reproducible from the PDF bytes; embeddings are already the only exception.

C  — Layout library / per-table parser

For

  • No reviewed data at all — new editions need no human.
  • Would have caught this page without an exclusion list.

Against

  • It infers the pairing, which is the exact mistake the Table 1 exclusions were created to avoid.
  • It is a new dependency in a repo whose extraction is deliberately one pdftotext call (extraction.py’s entire reason for existing).
  • It moves the review, it does not remove it. Someone still has to check the output of a heuristic; the difference is that they are checking code rather than the paper.

D  — Markdown tables in the artefact

For

  • One reviewed file per document, tables and prose together — the reviewer diffs one thing.
  • It is where the format is heading if the corpus grows beyond four documents.

Against

  • Format version 3: corpus.py currently says “everything else is text, verbatim”, and a Markdown table would become literal text with pipes if parsed today.
  • The landscape has no artefact at all (P5). This option makes the table fix wait for a conversion that is separately scheduled.
  • It contradicts the plan of record for exactly this slice.

E  — Exclude and publish the gap

For

  • Three lines of data, an existing golden, no new mechanism.
  • Stops the reordering, so the reader page shows the printed grid to a human.

Against

  • Measured: it fixes almost nothing. Still 8 chunks, still 6 branches, still cut mid-cell — raw -layout also puts R500 at the start of a line. Quotability stays at 1/5.
  • The fee question stays unanswerable, which is the question this document exists to answer.

That is the trigger to revisit D, and it is written into the PRD §12 so it is not a feeling.

Part 7 — My recommendation

A — reviewed rows, keyed by document and branch, with the reader changed and the front matter rescued

Concretely:

  1. TableRow becomes N cells, keeping substance/condition as the two-cell reading so Appendix 2 and 6a do not churn.
  2. The key becomes (doc_key, ref), where ref is section_ref when the document numbers its sections and the branch anchor otherwise — because the landscape is heading-only by design and synthetic refs would change the citation scheme.
  3. 8 rows across 3 tables, one chunk each, each cell pinned to its printed line.
  4. The reader gets labelled cells — the surface the defect was reported on.
  5. Pages 1–3 enter as reviewed front-matter passages, the same data-not-parser mechanism (Q6).
What would change my mind

On A → D: if the curator count passes about six tables, or if the landscape’s artefact conversion lands first — then the rows belong in the artefact and putting them in Python would be building tomorrow’s cleanup.

On B: if a model-drafted row plus a mandatory human diff proves faster to review than a human typing 8 rows, use it. That is D70.2’s fallback, and it is a drafting tool, not an ingest step.

On E: nothing. Measured, it fixes reading order and nothing else.

On the whole slice: if the landscape is judged a brochure rather than a governed document, the honest move is to drop it from the chat corpus and keep it as the reader page. I do not think that is right — the fee and seal facts are the questions — but it is the cheapest option and it should be rejected explicitly rather than by omission.
