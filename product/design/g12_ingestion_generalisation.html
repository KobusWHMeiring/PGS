Does this ingestion engine generalise past our four documents?

We hand-reviewed a canonical corpus for four documents we own. The next corpus will be documents we have never seen — different formatting, more tables, nastier tables. Four architectures, measured against the tables that already break today.

How to read this

This is a decision document. The question is not “which is best in the abstract” but what must be true of the engine so that a document we have not seen can be ingested without a human writing its tables by hand — and which of four architectures gets us there.

  1. Part 1 is the test set — eight table shapes, each one already in our own corpus, with what breaks. If an architecture cannot handle these, it does not generalise.
  2. Part 2 is what our current design actually is, stated plainly, including the part that does not scale.
  3. Part 3–5 are the four options with their strongest cases, the literature, and a matrix.
  4. Part 6 is one recommendation and what would change it. Part 7 is the control.

Read with g11_row_labels_exploration.html: G11 decides how one table’s labels reach a quote; this page decides whether that answer survives contact with the next corpus. Option E in G11 exists because of this page.

Part 2 — The shape of the problem: three concerns we have welded together

Our design answers three questions with one mechanism, and that is why it does not travel:

ConcernQuestion it answersOur mechanismScales?
Extractionhow do we get the structure out of the page? a hand-reviewed artefactno — a human per document
Verificationhow do we know the extraction is faithful? the same review, plus character-multiset tests written per document no — one test file per document
Citationwhat does a reader check, and how? a quote that must be a substring of the chunk partly — it works, but it forces context into the text
The weld

Because verification is “a human read it” and citation is “the quote is in the text”, anything a reader needs in order to understand a quote must be in the text. That is exactly what G11 is fighting. A general engine separates them: structure is verified against the source, and context is rendered from structure — not smuggled into the quote.

The measured cost of the weld

documented tables in the corpus:  4 artefact tables (14 rows) + 30 reviewed literals = 44 rows
reviewed by:                     a person, once, per document
test files guarding them:        test_corpus_landscape.py, test_corpus_opp.py, test_corpus_tables.py, test_tables.py
tables still mangled:            2 (Appendix 6a’s 20 rows, Appendix 7’s 4 chunks)

Four documents produced four bespoke test files and still left two tables broken. A fifth, unknown document would produce a fifth test file and no guarantee. That is the number that decides this.

Part 3 — The options

Four architectures, judged against Part 1. Names in brackets are the open-source or cloud components that already do most of the work — none of them is a recommendation to adopt a vendor.

X1. Keep transcription; repair the generator so it drafts tables too

The cheapest continuation: teach build_corpus.py to emit <!-- table --> blocks from the layout, and keep the human diff as the verification. Add Appendix 6a and 7 to the reviewed set.

Strongest case
  • No new dependency, no new claim, and it is D75’s already-agreed next step.
  • Keeps the strongest property we have: every line of the corpus has been read by a person.
  • Directly fixes the two live defects (6a, 7), which no architecture change does by itself.
Strongest case against
  • The human is still in the loop per document. Coverage does not become a mechanism.
  • Shape 3 (interleaving) is not a table-block problem — it needs structure recognition, which a line-based generator cannot do.
  • It is a dead end for “a corpus with more complex tables”, which is the stated requirement.

X2. Layout-aware extractor → our artefact IR → automated verification

Run a real document-structure model over the page — tables recovered with cells, spans, header rows and bounding boxes — and emit the artefact we already parse (cols:, row:, cell, row-divider). The human review stops being the only check: cell fidelity, row count and header association are asserted against the source text automatically. (Docling/TableFormer, Unstructured hi_res, Google Document AI layout parser, Azure Document Intelligence, LlamaParse — all produce exactly this.)

Strongest case
  • Our IR is already good: header, rows, cells, dividers, layout, legend. The work is in the producer, not in a rewrite.
  • Shapes 1, 2, 4, 5, 7 fall out of structure recognition — the header stops being an inference.
  • Verification becomes a test suite that any new document runs, not a review that one document needed.
  • Keeps the corpus quotable exactly as it is: cells stay the paper’s characters (D69).
Strongest case against
  • A new dependency and a new failure mode: an extractor’s confidence is not a review.
  • Shape 3 (interleaving) and shape 8 (artwork) still need a rule — extraction helps, it does not finish the job.
  • Costs an evaluation harness to know whether extractor A beats extractor B on our shapes, or it is a guess with a licence.

X3. Cite the region, not the string

A citation identifies (document, page, region or node, row) and the reader highlights it in the source — the NotebookLM-shaped UX. Provenance is kept per row at extraction time; the quote is what the model wrote, but what a reader verifies is the highlighted region.

today   citation = a string that must be a substring of a chunk
X3     citation = §2.3 / row “Below R 250,000” / page 2   →  rendered with the table’s own headings
                                and highlighted in the page image or the reader grid
Strongest case
  • Dissolves G11 rather than answering it: the labels are the region’s, so they never need to be in the quoted text.
  • It is the only model that survives every shape in Part 1 — including artwork in a cell and a row across a page break.
  • A reader verifying against the page is a stronger claim than a substring match.
Strongest case against
  • It is the largest change here: the audit’s meaning, the citation model, both templates, and the reader.
  • Regions need to survive re-ingest, exactly as anchors do (D68) — a new stability problem.
  • We would be building it for a corpus we do not yet have. D70.2’s discipline applies: do not build the fallback before the plan.

X4. Retrieve the page image and let a vision model read it

Skip extraction for the hard cases: index page images with a late-interaction model (ColPali and descendants) and let a multimodal model read the table at answer time.

Strongest case
  • Immune to every shape in Part 1 by construction: the page is the page.
  • No structure recognition to get wrong, and no artefact to keep in step.
Strongest case against
  • A quote stops being checkable. The whole product claim is that every sentence is traceable to characters; a vision model reading a picture gives characters nobody verified.
  • D70.2 already decided this class: a model’s output is a fallback that a human reviews, not the plan.
  • Another model call per page, and a new provider dependency on the answer path.

Where the field disagrees with itself: retrieval research optimises the index (context on the vector), while document-QA research optimises the reading unit (a cell needs its header). Our audit optimises the quote. X2 satisfies the first two; X3 satisfies the third. The disagreement is only resolvable by separating the three, which is Part 2’s point.

Part 6 — Recommendation

Recommended

X2 now, X3 when a document demands it, X1 in the meantime, X4 never as the plan.

X2 is the step that changes the coverage model from “a person reviewed this document” to “a suite checks every document”. It fits what we already have — the artefact IR is the right shape for a structure model’s output — and it fixes the two live defects (Appendix 6a’s 20 headerless rows, Appendix 7’s interleaved chunks) as a side effect of the harness rather than as another hand review.

X3 is the destination, and only one thing justifies building it: a document whose tables cannot be flattened into our IR without losing what a reader needs. Until one arrives, G11’s option E is the same idea in miniature — context rendered, never quoted — and it is cheap enough to prove the UX question (“can a reader tell context from quotation?”) before X3 bets the reader on it.

X1 is not wasted time: repairing the generator (D75) is a prerequisite for X2 anyway — an extractor that cannot emit our IR has nowhere to put its tables. Do it as X2’s first slice, not as the plan.

Order of work: (1) build the Part 1 test set as fixtures — eight shapes, asserted, red; (2) evaluate two extractors against it (Docling and Document AI are the two with published table structure; measure cells, headers, spans, and speed); (3) make the generator emit table blocks from the winner; (4) the automated verification suite (cell fidelity, row count, header association) as the gate for any new document; (5) re-review Appendix 6a and 7 through it.

What would change my mind: if the test set shows extractors lose spans or qualifiers on more than two of the eight shapes, then X3 moves first — a region citation does not need the structure to be perfect, only locatable. And if a deployment must ingest a document this week, X1 plus G11-E is the honest answer, with the review written down as debt.

The cost nobody budgets for

Every option except X2 makes the verification problem worse, and verification is the one thing this product sells. An extractor with no harness is a faster way to be wrong; a vision model is a faster way to be unfalsifiable. Whatever is chosen, the Part 1 fixtures are the first commit.
